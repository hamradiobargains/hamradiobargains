About the Kenwood R-1000

The Kenwood (Trio) R-1000 is a receive-only general-coverage communications receiver made in Japan from 1979 into the mid-1980s, covering 200 kHz to 30 MHz in AM wide, AM narrow, USB, LSB and CW. It was a landmark at its price: PLL-synthesized tuning with a five-digit digital display plus an analog dial, a built-in clock/timer that can switch the set (and a recorder) on and off, a noise blanker, a step attenuator running to −60 dB, and three stock IF filters — roughly 12 kHz wide AM, 6 kHz narrow AM, and a 2.7 kHz SSB filter. Reviewers who have lived with the whole Kenwood receiver line single this one out for its audio: a large internal speaker and that wide filter make shortwave broadcast listening genuinely pleasant, which is not something said about most synthesized receivers of the era. It suits the listener first: shortwave broadcast, utility and ham-band monitoring from a solid, repairable, no-menu box. It does not transmit, it has no FM mode, and it has no memories in the modern sense — you tune it, which is the point for the people who seek one out. Buying used, work through the age faults, not the features. The detailed owner checklists for this model start with the power supply: check the big supply capacitor (C209, 4700 µF), look for cold solder joints around the supply and regulators, and note that many units were wired with the AC fuse in the neutral — a safety item a tech should correct. Sensitivity as stock is poor on longwave and mediumwave (there is a known modification that removes the factory pads), so a unit that seems deaf below 1.6 MHz may be stock, not broken — but a unit that is deaf on the shortwave bands has a real problem. Cabinet paint chips easily and the locking carrying handle (which doubles as a tilt stand) is often missing. Our ledger's spread tells the story: tested, clean units make the $200 median and above; vague "WORKS" listings sit at the bottom near $150. Pay for demonstrated reception on several bands, not for a lit display.

Common questions

I just got an R-1000 and it hears almost nothing but static — is it broken? (closely paraphrased from a real RadioReference forum post by a new owner)

Usually not, and the usual cause is the antenna and the band, not the radio. The R-1000 has a sensitive front end, but HF is not scanning: above roughly 10 MHz is daytime territory and below it is nighttime territory, and a random short mobile whip indoors will mostly deliver noise. Put a real length of wire on it, outdoors if you can, pick a band that is open for the time of day, open the attenuator, and try a strong broadcast station first. If it is still silent everywhere with a decent antenna, then start on the checkout in Q3.

Why is my R-1000 so deaf on longwave and mediumwave compared with shortwave?

Because Kenwood built it that way. Stock units carry attenuation pads in the LW/MW path, and the detailed reviews call the stock LW/MW sensitivity "pretty dismal" while shortwave sensitivity is fine. There is a documented modification that removes the MW/LW RF and IF pads and restores the low bands, at the cost of more front-end overload on big antennas (the step attenuator is the counterweight). A seller describing weak mediumwave on an unmodified unit is describing a stock R-1000.

What should be checked before putting a newly bought R-1000 into daily use?

The owner checkout literature for this exact model lists: the fuse wiring (many were wired with the AC fuse in the neutral — have it corrected), the power-supply capacitor C209 (4700 µF / 25 V) and cold solder joints on the supply board, and two 1000 µF capacitors (C186 and C191) on the main receiver board. None of that makes the R-1000 a money pit — it is a simple, well-documented radio — but a unit that has had that work done is worth more than one that has never been opened, and the questions cost nothing to ask.
