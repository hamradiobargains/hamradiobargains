About the Yaesu FT-857

The Yaesu FT-857 is the original all-band, all-mode mobile — HF, 6 meters, 2 meters, and 70cm in one compact box, 100 watts on HF and 6m, 50 watts on 2m, and 20 watts on 70cm. It was the mobile sibling of the FT-897, sharing its design, and for years it was the default answer to "one radio that does everything in the truck, then comes inside for Field Day." The control head separates from the body, it runs SSB, CW, AM, FM, and digital modes, and it will work the world on HF and still check into the local repeater on the drive home. What makes the original 857 worth separating from the much more common FT-857D is exactly what to check before you price one. On the standard account — Yaesu's own FT-857D manual, which names the difference directly — the D added the DSP-2 digital signal processing unit and 60-meter coverage; an original 857 has neither unless a previous owner fitted the DSP-2 himself, and ledger sales show DSP-fitted originals priced at the top of the range for exactly that reason. Our recorded sales put fair value around $850 on 14 in-window sales (range $549.69–$1,200), so a genuine bargain sits under about $638 — but compare like with like: a DSP-fitted, boxed radio and a bare unit-only radio are not the same deal. Buying used, test it the way it will be used. Run full power on HF and listen hard: the documented family fault is deterioration of the 455 kHz ceramic IF filter, which owners report as S9-plus static crashes (with or without an antenna), worsening noise, and in advanced cases loss of HF SSB output — a radio doing that is a repair, not a bargain. Also know about the ALC/power-spike behaviour at low power settings (spikes above 100W on the first syllable when set to 10–20W) — harmless barefoot, a real risk when driving an amplifier. Work the display: the zebra-strip LCD connection is a known age fault (dropping lines/segments; cleaning and reseating can help, board replacement is the durable fix). The menu system is deep enough that a seller who "never figured it out" may be selling a healthy radio cheap — or hiding a fault behind the menus. Confirm the separation cable, the MH-31 hand mic, and the power lead are in the box, and ask directly whether it has been opened up for MARS/CAP or a fan mod.

Common questions

What's the difference between the FT-857 and FT-857D?

The D is the later version of the same radio, with formerly optional extras made standard: the DSP-2 digital signal processing unit, the TCXO-9 high-stability oscillator, and pre-programmed US 60-meter (5 MHz) channels (QST's Nov 2004 review of the D models states all three). The original covered 160m–70cm without 60m, with DSP-2 and TCXO-9 as extra-cost options. So DSP alone does NOT prove a unit is a D — some originals were fitted with it; the 60m channel memories are the practical test. Ledger sales flag DSP-fitted units, and they price higher — so ask, don't assume.

Does the FT-857 have a built-in antenna tuner?

No. Neither the 857 nor the 857D has an internal tuner; owners pair them with an external tuner such as the Yaesu FC-30 (which mounts alongside the rig and is controlled from it) or a third-party auto-tuner. Budget for one if your antennas aren't resonant.

Can the FT-857 be used as a base station, or is it mobile only?

Both — that's the point of the rig. It needs an external 13.8V supply (Yaesu specifies 22A at full power; owners commonly cite a 25A-class supply) and owners run it as a compact base, a mobile with the head separated, and a portable/Field Day radio. Check full HF output before buying, whichever job you have in mind.
