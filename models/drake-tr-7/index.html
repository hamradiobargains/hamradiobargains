About the Drake TR-7

The Drake TR-7 was Drake's leap into solid-state: a broadband SSB / CW / AM (and RTTY) HF transceiver from Miamisburg with general-coverage receive — 1.5–30 MHz continuous, and below 1.5 MHz at reduced performance with the DR-7 board — at a time when most HF rigs still worked in crystal-bound 500 kHz slices. It did it with up-conversion (a 48 MHz first IF), a high-level double-balanced mixer front end with no RF amplifier stage, true passband tuning, and a permeability-tuned VFO inside a tracking synthesizer, so the readout does not drift the way a free-running carrier oscillator would. The standard selectivity is a 2.3 kHz SSB filter, with other bandwidths as plug-in options. Reviewers of the design still call it overbuilt and "almost impossible to kill," with a quiet, overload-resistant receiver and rich transmit audio. Set your power expectations from the design, not from a modern 100-watt label. The transmitter was designed for 250 watts input, which works out to roughly 130–150 watts output on the lower bands and about 90–100 watts on 15 and 10 meters in the well-known Drake documentation — and the PA is conservatively rated well above that. It is also a hungry radio: about 3 amps on receive and 22–25 amps on transmit in the same documentation, which is why the matching PS-7 supply is part of almost every complete station, and why size and current rule out casual mobile use. The red LED readout washing out in sunlight is the other period compromise owners name. Buying used, separate the radio from the station before you price anything — the ledger's top two sales are PS-7 bundles, and a bare rig plus a supply you still have to buy is a different total. Then work the known weak spots. Output on the high bands: a documented owner repair started at 40 watts on 10 meters with power collapsing to zero after a minute of CW, traced into the PA driver stage — so transmit on every band, sustained, into a dummy load, and watch it hold. The supply: owners report PS-7 shutdowns on transmit that clear after removing AC power for about ten seconds (see FAQ 2) — a rig that does that on a dummy load has a real fault; a rig that only does it next to a near-field antenna may be showing you an RF-in-the-shack problem instead. Identity matters too: very early TR-7s were sold without the DR-7 digital readout / general-coverage board (few were made, and the documentation says a TR-7 is worth significantly less without it), and the later TR-7A made the noise blanker and filters standard that were options on the early radio — ask which filters and whether the blanker are actually fitted, and whether the FA-7 cooling fan is included for heavy-duty modes. Deep service needs the service manual and extender boards, which are no longer available from Drake — a documented, recently serviced unit earns its premium here.

Common questions

"How much power does a TR-7 actually make — and why is mine (or the one I'm looking at) low on 10 meters?"

Expect roughly 130–150 watts on the lower bands and about 90–100 watts on 15 and 10 meters from a healthy radio (250 watts input design, per the Drake documentation collected by VE3EFJ). Noticeably less than that on the high bands, or power that fades to zero after a short CW transmission, is a documented PA-stage fault pattern, not "they all do that": one owner's detailed repair found low drive and thermal trouble in the driver / pre-driver stage and restored full output on 10 meters after replacing driver devices and correcting bias (his measured bias was 2.2 A against a suggested few hundred milliamps). Price a low-on-10 unit as a PA repair until a sustained dummy-load test proves otherwise.

"My TR-7 and PS-7 shut off on transmit — is the power supply dying?"

That is a real owner question, asked in exactly that form on the Drake group: a TR-7 / PS-7 that shuts down on 75 m at anywhere from 25 to 120 watts, revives after unplugging for about ten seconds, yet runs full power (that owner reported 150 watts) into a dummy load without tripping. Group answers point first at RF getting into the PS-7's shutdown circuit — the supply removes power from its regulator when it trips — with bypass-capacitor modifications for the TR-7 / PS-7 as the documented countermeasure, and antenna near-field placement as the aggravating factor in that case. For a buyer, the dummy-load test is the divider: trips on a dummy load = fault in the rig / supply; trips only on a nearby antenna = an RF-environment problem you may inherit with your own shack layout.

"What is the DR-7, and what about the TR-7A — which am I actually buying?"

The DR-7 is the digital readout / general-coverage board. The operating manual's specifications are written around it, and the Drake documentation notes the very early TR-7 was sold without it — few were made, and "a TR7 is significantly less without the DR7 display." The TR-7A (around 1982) is not a different radio: it is a TR-7 with equipment that was optional on the early model — noise blanker and crystal filters — fitted as standard. So the used-buying question is never just "TR-7 or TR-7A?"; it is "DR-7 fitted? Which optional filters are in it? Noise blanker fitted?" — asked of the specific unit, because an early, optionless radio and a loaded late radio share a front panel and not a value.
