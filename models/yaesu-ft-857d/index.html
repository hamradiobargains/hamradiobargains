About the Yaesu FT-857D

The FT-857D is the "D" revision of Yaesu's shack-in-a-box mobile: HF and 6 meters at 100 watts, 2 meters at 50 watts, 70 cm at 20 watts, all modes, in what Yaesu billed as the world's smallest full-power HF/VHF/UHF package — 155 × 52 × 233 mm, with a detachable front panel for remote mounting. The D matters, and Leo has already ruled (2026-10-10) that the 857 and 857D stay separate radios in our system: the D brought the DSP unit and 60-meter coverage into the standard package where the plain 857 left more of that to options. Receive coverage runs from 100 kHz through the FM broadcast and air bands, there are 200 memories, and the whole thing draws about 22 amps at full power, so it wants real wiring, not a cigarette-lighter plug. Production ended around 2019–2020 and Yaesu shipped no direct successor — the FT-891 that followed it dropped VHF and UHF entirely — which is a large part of why clean 857Ds hold their money. It suits the ham who wants one radio that does HF DX from the truck, 2-meter repeaters on the drive, and portable work off a battery, and who will trade menu-diving on a small screen for that coverage. If you only ever work HF, newer radios do that job with better displays for less grief. Buying used, two age faults lead every owner discussion. First, the display: vertical "zebra stripes" — missing lines in the LCD — are the model's signature failure, blamed on the aging flex-cable adhesive between the display and the head board, and worse on units that lived in hot cars. A striped display is usable but it is a discount, and heat-based home fixes are temporary by most accounts. Second, the finals: test output on every band into a dummy load if you can, especially VHF/UHF on a hard-run mobile. Then inventory the deal against the median before you judge it — our kept sales include bare radios near $810–$825 and bundles with a YSK-857 separation kit, an LDG tuner, or a Signalink pushing $1,100–$1,500. Those are not the same product, and the $1,049 median already has bundle money inside it.

Common questions

What is the difference between the FT-857 and the FT-857D? (a real, recurring buyer question — asked verbatim in buying guides and forums)

They are different radios with different standard packages, not region twins. The D fits the DSP unit as standard and adds 60-meter coverage; the plain 857 left the DSP as an option. Output (100/50/20 W), size, the detachable panel, and the general design are shared. Price a plain 857 against 857 money and an 857D against the $1,049 median here — do not let a seller blur them.

My FT-857(D) display has zebra stripes / missing lines — what causes it, and can it be fixed?

Owner teardown reports trace it to the flex cable that joins the LCD to the control-head board: the adhesive/conductive joint ages and loses contact, line by line, and heat makes it worse — several owners tie it directly to hot-car life. Careful heat-and-pressure re-seating of that joint is documented and can improve a display, sometimes only partly and not always permanently; the certain fix is a replacement display or head. For a buyer: stripes are a price cut, not a deal-breaker, and a seller's "it comes and goes with the weather" is the same fault introducing itself.

Is the FT-857D still a good buy compared with the FT-891?

That comparison is asked constantly because the 891 is the newer, cheaper-feeling answer — until you read the band list. The FT-891 is HF/6 m only, with a bigger display and more modern DSP. The 857D is the only one of the two that also does 2 m and 70 cm all-mode. If your operating is HF-only, the 891 is the easier long-term buy; if you need the VHF/UHF side — satellites, weak-signal, or just one radio in the truck — the discontinued 857D is still the tool, which is exactly why its used prices stay up.
