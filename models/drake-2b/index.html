About the Drake 2B

The Drake 2B is a tube HF receiver from R.L. Drake of Miamisburg, Ohio — early 1960s, ham-bands receive in crystal-controlled segments, SSB/CW/AM, and the radio that taught a generation of Novices and Generals what a good receiver sounded like. It is a receiver only — there is no transmitter in the box — and it was built to be paired with a separate transmitter, or to simply listen, which is what a lot of owners still do with it. Its calling cards are the smooth passband tuning, a product detector that made SSB genuinely pleasant on a budget rig, and Drake build quality in a compact steel cabinet. What it's known for, sixty years on, is selectivity and audio that shame a lot of newer radios, in a box that a competent bench can keep alive almost indefinitely: tubes, point-to-point-era construction, and a design that has been documented, restored, and written about for decades. The accessories are part of the value story. The matching 2-BQ speaker / Q-multiplier and the 2-AC crystal calibrator were options when new, and they still separate prices today — in our ledger, the two top sales ($299.95 and $349.99) were explicitly option-loaded units, flagged as outliers against a fair value around $204 on 9 in-window sales, with bare receivers trading mostly between about $150 and $230. A genuine bargain on a bare, working 2B sits under about $153; a "bargain" that includes the speaker/Q-multiplier and calibrator needs to be judged as a bundle, not against the bare-radio median. Buying used, assume age until proven otherwise. A 2B that "powers on, untested" — one ledger sale was sold in exactly those words at $150 — is a project: electrolytic capacitors, tired tubes, a scratchy band switch, and dial-cord / calibration drift are the normal restoration list, and none of them is exotic, but all of them are time or money. Ask whether it has been recapped, whether it actually receives on every band segment (extra crystals were how you got full segment coverage — a stock unit may not cover everything you expect), whether the passband tuning and Q-multiplier (if fitted) work, and whether the S-meter and calibrator behave. Cosmetically clean, electrically restored, accessory-complete 2Bs are the top of this market for a reason.

Common questions

Is the Drake 2B a transceiver?

No — it's a receiver only. It was designed to pair with a separate transmitter (owners commonly ran it with period Drake and other transmitters), or as a standalone listening receiver. If you want to transmit, budget for a transmitter and a T/R arrangement as a separate purchase.

What are the 2-BQ and 2-AC, and do they matter to the price?

Yes. The 2-BQ is the matching speaker with a built-in Q-multiplier (a selectivity aid), and the 2-AC is the crystal calibrator. Both were extra-cost options. In our recorded sales the option-loaded units are the top prices and are flagged as outliers against the bare-radio fair value — so a listing with both accessories is not overpriced just because it sits above the bare 2B median, and a bare receiver shouldn't be priced like a loaded one.

Having some issues with weak/no response crystals in the Drake 2B… I've tried two 18 MHz [crystals] that won't oscillate in circuit… Has anyone tried increasing the gain of the xtal osc stage to get weaker xtals to work?

Usually no redesign is needed. The 20m-and-up crystals are series-resonant 3rd-overtone types that only oscillate when their resonating coil is tuned — do the manual's Crystal Oscillator Alignment (L9/L10) with a high-impedance meter, clean the band switch and crystal sockets, and suspect the crystal itself: the poster's replacement 18 MHz crystal "took right off" at roughly −6V oscillator reading versus −2V for the weak ones. Aged band crystals going weak or intermittent — and missing crystals for the extra 10m segments — are common on used 2Bs, which is why "dead band" on this receiver is often a crystal, not a front end. The same list's restoration threads add the era's other usual suspects: a 60-year-old receiver that has never been recapped is the norm (failed can electrolytics → hum, low audio, a dead diode detector), and the 6U8 oscillator tube is the usual suspect when a band oscillator won't run at all.
