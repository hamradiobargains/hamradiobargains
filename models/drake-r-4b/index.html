About the Drake R-4B

The Drake R-4B (1967-1973) is boatanchor royalty -- the receiver half of Drake's legendary 4-line, built in Miamisburg, Ohio, when radios were furniture and built like it. This is a hobby within the hobby: tuning is by PTO, a permeability-tuned oscillator you spin by hand, and a healthy one glides like it is on ball bearings. Spin one that has been sitting for thirty years and you will feel the famous gravel -- dried lubricant and end-play that a proper servicing puts right, and the first thing any buyer should check. The R-4B covers HF in 500 kHz slices set by plug-in band crystals (80, 40, 20, 15, and part of 10 meters came standard; the rest is a scavenger hunt, with new crystals costing real money today). What it does not need is fear: the old hands love how easy these are to work on, and unlike some collectible receivers it does not suffer the dreaded 'deadly cap' syndrome. Assume it needs a capacitor recap unless the seller documents one, listen for hum that says the filter caps are done, and zero-beat the 100 kHz calibrator across the bands to check the dial. Originality matters -- extra holes and mystery jacks kill collector value. Pair it with a T-4XB transmitter and the MS-4 speaker and you have the classic American station, glowing warmly and pulling signals like it is 1970.

Common questions

What is the PTO, and why does everyone talk about it?

The permeability-tuned oscillator is the R-4B's VFO -- a precision mechanical assembly you tune by hand. When the lubricant dries out and end-play drifts, tuning feels gritty or gravelly instead of silky, and it is the number-one thing to check on a used unit. The good news: it is a documented, doable service, not a death sentence. A silky PTO is worth paying for; a gravelly one is a bargaining chip.

Do I need to worry about the band crystals?

The R-4B only receives the 500 kHz slices its plug-in crystals cover, so check which crystals come with it -- tune to each band's edges and listen. Dead or missing crystals are a classic gotcha, and replacements from crystal houses cost real money each today. The stock set covers 80, 40, 20, 15, and part of 10 meters; WARC bands and shortwave segments mean buying more. Factor missing crystals into your offer.

Tube radio -- is it not a maintenance nightmare?

Less than you would think, and that is the R-4B's reputation: straightforward circuits, no unobtainium parts, and a design the old hands call easy to work on. Budget for the standard age items -- electrolytic recap, contact cleaner on every switch and pot, dial lamps, maybe tubes -- and bring it up on a variac if it has been stored for years. Ask the seller what has been done; a documented recap and alignment is worth real money. What kills value is not age but hacks: drilled holes and clipped leads from decades of mods.
